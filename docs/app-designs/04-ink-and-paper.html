Is Sola Scriptura a Valid Doctrine?

Side A: Sola Scriptura  —  Side B: Scripture + Tradition
By TheologianMark · 3 days ago · Soteriology Ecclesiology Authority · Open
The doctrine of Sola Scriptura — that Scripture alone is the final authority in matters of faith and practice — has been a cornerstone of Protestant theology since the Reformation. Critics argue that the early Church relied on both written Scripture and oral apostolic tradition, and that the canon itself was determined by Church councils. This debate invites careful examination of the biblical, historical, and theological evidence on both sides.

ReformedPastor Side A Tier III Exegetical Argument 2 days ago
Second Timothy 3:16–17 states that Scripture is “God-breathed” and sufficient to make the man of God “thoroughly equipped for every good work.” If Scripture itself claims sufficiency, then adding tradition as a co-equal authority undermines the Bible’s own testimony about itself. The Bereans in Acts 17:11 were commended precisely because they tested apostolic teaching against Scripture alone.

PatristicScholar Side B Tier IV Historical Rebuttal 2 days ago
The 2 Timothy passage affirms Scripture’s inspiration and usefulness but does not explicitly claim it is the sole authority. Paul himself, in 2 Thessalonians 2:15, commands believers to “hold to the traditions” whether taught “by word of mouth or by letter.” The early Church Fathers — Irenaeus, Basil, Vincent of Lérins — consistently appealed to tradition alongside Scripture.

ReformedPastor Side A Tier III Counter-Rebuttal 1 day ago
The “traditions” Paul refers to in 2 Thessalonians were the apostles’ own teaching, not the later accumulated traditions of the medieval church. Once apostolic teaching was inscripturated, the written form became the normative standard. Appealing to patristic writers actually supports this — many Fathers elevated Scripture as the supreme rule of faith.

EcumenicalBridge Neutral Tier II Synthesis 1 day ago
Both sides make compelling points. Perhaps the question is not whether tradition has any role, but whether it has binding authority equal to Scripture. Even within Protestantism, confessions and creeds function as interpretive tradition — Sola Scriptura has never meant “no tradition at all,” but rather that Scripture is the final court of appeal.
